If paying is the right answer

Pay in the first five days and it costs 30% less

The discount runs from the day you were notified, not the day you drove into the zone. Most people spend those five days trying to work out what the letter says, and lose the discount to confusion.

Check these three things first The plate, the date and time, and the make of the car. If any of them is wrong, do not pay — you have a ground, and paying is an admission.

Where the payment details are

Every official fine carries its own payment section: the amount, the reduced amount, the deadline for each, and the payment references for the issuing city. Pay against those references and quote the file number from the letter. Do not pay a third-party site that offers to “handle” it for a fee on top.

Watch out for look-alike letters

Because these fines are known to arrive long after the trip, they are a popular target for fake demands. A real one names the city that issued it, the exact location and time of the entry, and a file number you can quote back. If a letter asks you to pay to a private company's account without any of that, treat it as a scam until proven otherwise.

What happens if you ignore it

The amount increases, and the debt does not disappear when you leave the country. Collection across EU borders is routine, and the letters keep coming. Ignoring is the one option that reliably costs more than every other option.
